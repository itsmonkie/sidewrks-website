I haven't written here since August. Not because nothing happened. Because I started to doubt the thing this site is about.

I've always built things. Some I gave away, some were attempts at businesses, and most were both at different points. Building was never the question. It was the fixed part, the thing everything else hung off.

## Everyone can build now

Over the last year that's moved. I see more and more people knocking tools together, quickly and competently enough, with Claude doing most of the work. Not engineers. People who not long ago would have gone looking for an app, or asked someone like me. Now they just make the thing. The more I watched, the harder it got to explain why anyone should use something I'd built rather than build their own.

The argument against is simple. Anything I make can be copied in an afternoon. Anyone with a problem can describe it and get a working answer. Often the need for a product disappears entirely, because the model just does the job. Non-builders can now build, and given the option, that's what they spend their time doing.

I'm not outside this. I built Drill Room, a tool for running incident response tabletop exercises, in evenings and weekends with an agent fleet doing most of the work. The tools that made that possible are the same tools that make it easy for someone else to build their own. I can't have one without the other.

## Where the depth is

But I don't think the argument holds all the way.

What I notice most is how quickly people now jump to building. The instinct used to be to look for something that already existed, or to ask. Now it's to open Claude.

I watched someone set out to build a whole partner portal this way, on their own, without involving the people who own the product it was meant to sit on. It wasn't a bad idea, and Claude would happily have built it. But a partner portal isn't a set of screens. It touches accounts, permissions, data that lives elsewhere, and commitments other teams have already made to those partners. None of that is visible from inside a chat window.

That's the part that worries me. Problems usually look simpler than they are, and the hardest parts are often the connections: to the systems already running, the processes people already follow, the decisions already made. Build in isolation and you don't just get a fragile tool. You get a second version of the truth, and an organisation that's a little less coherent than it was.

Drill Room has the same shape. A naïve version would generate a scenario and print a report. The part that matters is quieter. Nothing logged in a session can be edited afterwards, anything added later is marked as added later, and actions carry into the next year's exercise. An auditor cares about that. Nobody asks for it until the auditor does. None of it came from code. It came from having been on the receiving end of those questions.

## What was valuable all along

So I still think there's value in building something well. I'm just less sure the value was ever in the building.

What people used, and trusted, was judgement: knowing which problem was worth solving, where the depth was, and what would break. Code was where that judgement became visible. Writing it was hard enough that the two looked like the same thing. Now the code is cheap, and what's left is the part that was doing the work all along.

## The part I loved

That's a reasonable conclusion. It's also an uncomfortable one, because the code was the part I loved.

A big piece of the satisfaction was always use. Using what I'd made, and seeing other people use it. That feels thinner now, partly because anyone could have made it, and partly because I made less of it myself. Building with Claude is highly effective. It's also less satisfying than building was before, and pretending otherwise wouldn't help anyone.

I don't have a neat answer for where this ends up, for me or for people like me. I'm still building. Drill Room exists because of these tools, and I'm glad it does. But I've stopped assuming the building is the valuable part. Maybe it never was, and I enjoyed it enough not to check.
